I finished the [Beast of Big Creek Skyrace](https://www.beastofbigcreek.com/) in 5:04:34. That put me 45th out of 51 women, and 144th out of 160 finishers overall.

Six women finished behind me. I want to tell you why I'm writing a blog post about that anyway.

## The Warm-Up Laps

Here's the moment I knew what kind of race I'd signed up for.

The Beast is 24 km with 5,300 feet of climbing, straight up to the summit of Mount Ellinor and back down. That is not a course you warm up for. That is a course you save everything for.

![The start line at Big Creek, under the trees](/images/2026-08-01/start.jpg)

And yet, standing in that crowd, I watched runner after runner go jogging past me — *fast* — doing warm-up laps. Loosening up. Striding out. Checking their legs before a race that was about to take them 5,000 vertical feet into the Olympics.

I was standing still, trying to conserve every calorie I had.

That told me everything. People who warm up before a mountain race like this one aren't planning to survive it. They're planning to race it.

## Who Actually Shows Up in Hoodsport

Hoodsport, Washington has a population of a few hundred people. It's a small town on the Hood Canal where you'd go for oysters and a quiet weekend. It does not sound like an international race destination.

But the Beast is a [Merrell Skyrunner World Series](https://ultrasignup.com/results_event.aspx?did=134623) stop, with seventeen years of history running the steep trails to Ellinor's summit. So look at who came:

1. **Eve Pannone**, GBR, team Brooks — 2:26:50
2. Natalie Beadle, GBR, team Arc'teryx — 2:35:13
3. Claudia Corral, ESP, team Scarpa — 2:36:15
4. Carmen Graves, USA, team Merrell — 2:36:58
5. Kalie McCrystal, CAN — 2:37:25

![The Merrell Skyrunner arch — an international series finish line in a Washington state park](/images/2026-08-01/finish_arch.jpg)

Great Britain, Spain, Canada. Brooks, Arc'teryx, Scarpa, Merrell, Oiselle. Spanish and Mexican and British Columbian entrants scattered through the field. The men's winner, Morgan Elliott, ran 2:05:27 — for 15 miles and 5,300 feet of climbing.

These are people whose job is running.

## What 45th of 51 Actually Means

So here's the part I find genuinely interesting, and it's why I went and pulled the full results instead of just looking at the top ten.

The median woman in this race finished in **3:32:55**. I ran 5:04:34. I was an hour and a half behind the middle of the field, and more than twice the winner's time.

But look at *who* the field was:

- 37 of the 51 women were under 40. That's 73% of the field.
- Exactly **five** women in the entire race were 50 or older.
- The oldest woman to finish was 56. I'm 55.

I was the second-oldest woman on that course. Of the five of us over 50, I finished third.

I'm not going to pretend that makes me fast — it doesn't, and a five-person age group isn't much of a podium. But it does mean the number "45th" is measuring something different than it looks like it's measuring. I wasn't 45th out of 51 recreational runners. I was 45th out of 51 people in a field that was mostly sponsored athletes and women half my age, on a course hard enough that people came from three continents to run it.

That's a different sentence than "I was near the back."

## The Climb Itself

The course goes up. That's really the whole description.

I put together a preview of the section from mile 2.7 to the Ellinor summit, which is the part that decides your day:

{{< youtube P9FlCtXN-Uo >}}

You can see why the times spread out the way they do. I had the grade on screen while I was filming, and there are long stretches sitting at **40%**. That is not runnable climbing for most of us — it's hands-on-knees, pick-a-line, count-to-fifty-and-look-up climbing. The kind where the person ahead of you is directly above your head.

![Old growth disappearing into the mist on the way up](/images/2026-08-01/misty_trees.jpg)

We started in sunshine down in the trees. But the thing about climbing a mountain is that you don't wait for the weather to come to you — you go up into it. Somewhere on that ascent the light went flat, the forest filled with mist, and the temperature dropped.

![Looking down off the ridge into the cloud](/images/2026-08-01/ridge_drop.jpg)

By the time the trees gave out and the route turned to rock and scree, we were climbing in and out of cloud.

![The summit ridge](/images/2026-08-01/summit_ridge.jpg)

I won't oversell the summit views, because we didn't really get them. What we got was better in a way I didn't expect: clouds tearing across a jagged ridgeline, the Olympics showing themselves for a few seconds at a time and then closing up again, and the Hood Canal somewhere far below under a lid of fog.

![Glimpses of the Hood Canal through the clouds](/images/2026-08-01/summit_clouds.jpg)

Then you turn around and go back down, on legs that just spent 5,000 feet earning that.

Only 3 people out of 163 starters didn't finish. Everybody else just kept going up.

## Hoodsport, and the Oysters

Here is the thing nobody tells you about picking races in small towns: the recovery is better.

![The Hood Canal at the end of the day](/images/2026-08-01/hood_canal_pier.jpg)

Hoodsport sits right on the Hood Canal, which is one of the great oyster places on earth — cold, clean water, and oysters that taste like it. The town knows exactly what it is.

![The town knows what it's about](/images/2026-08-01/hood_canal_mural.jpg)

After five hours on Mount Ellinor, sitting down at a picnic table by the water in front of a plate of them felt like the most reasonable thing I have ever done.

![Picnic shelters by the water](/images/2026-08-01/oyster_shacks.jpg)

![Raw, on ice, with mignonette](/images/2026-08-01/oysters_raw.jpg)

We got them raw first, and then grilled, which if you have never had them that way is worth the trip on its own — hot, buttery, garlicky, with bread to mop the shell out.

![Grilled, with bread for the juice](/images/2026-08-01/oysters_grilled.jpg)

If you run this race, stay the night. Don't drive home the same day. The whole trip is better than the race alone, and the race is already good.

## Would I Do It Again

Yes.

I'll be honest that seeing my name that far down a results page stung for a minute. It always does. But I've been doing this long enough to know the difference between a race that made me slow and a race that was just *fast*, and this was the second one. I got to stand on a start line with athletes I'd otherwise only read about, watch them warm up for a race I was scared of, and then go climb the same mountain they climbed. Slower. In the same clouds.

Five hours and four minutes. Second-oldest woman out there. Fifty-one of us started up Ellinor and fifty-one of us came back down.

I'll take it.
